\title[MapSORT]{MapSORT: Landmark Association for Identity-Persistent Tracking of Stationary Crops and Fruit}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Srihari} \sur{Vemuru}}\email{vemshari27@gmail.com}

\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{\tbd}, \orgname{\tbd}, \orgaddress{\city{\tbd}, \country{\tbd}}}

\abstract{\textbf{Purpose:} Plant- and fruit-level records in precision agriculture, such as yield estimates, phenotyping logs and targeted spraying, require every plant or fruit to keep one identity across a video, including when the camera leaves and later returns to it. General-purpose multi-object trackers lose identity in exactly this situation, because crops look alike and an object out of view for more than a few frames is forgotten. This work turns the property that sets agricultural targets apart, that they do not move, into an association cue that keeps identities intact across occlusions and revisits.

\textbf{Methods:} MapSORT reconstructs the scene with structure-from-motion and gives every detection and every track a landmark: a Gaussian over the object's position in the scene map. Built on BoT-SORT, it fits a camera to its own tracks in every frame, from their landmarks and boxes, and uses that camera to place each stationary object in the image. Tracks the frame-to-frame association misses are re-matched where their landmark projects; lost tracks, and tracks that left the view long ago and were kept dormant rather than deleted, are revived the same way, including objects that re-enter at the frame edge. MapSORT is compared with StrongSORT, Deep OC-SORT and BoT-SORT on four public datasets (LettuceMOTS, MOrangeT, GrapeMOTS, AppleMOTS) and on per-tree yield counts in a mango orchard.

\textbf{Results:} Averaged over the four datasets, MapSORT reaches 95.5 HOTA and 96.0 IDF1, 10.3 and 16.7 points above the strongest baseline, BoT-SORT, and cuts identity switches from 1154, the fewest of any baseline, to 217. It preserves identity in 92.0\% of 1043 revisit events, against at most 12.5\% for the baselines, at every length of absence. It has fewer identity switches than every baseline on 19 of the 21 test sequences. Per-tree mango counts are within \tbd{}\% of the ground-truth yield.

\textbf{Conclusion:} The position of a stationary object in a map of the scene is an identity cue that survives arbitrarily long absences. Recovering the camera from the tracks themselves turns that position into a motion model and a re-identification cue, giving identity-consistent tracks and accurate counts across crops, platforms and viewing patterns.}

\keywords{Multi-object tracking, Structure-from-motion, Camera resection, Fruit counting, Plant re-identification, Yield estimation}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Many precision-agriculture tasks operate at the level of the individual plant or fruit. Yield estimation from video counts fruit by counting tracks \citep{stein2016mango,liu2019monocular,hani2020comparative,villacres2023apple}. Per-plant phenotyping attaches measurements from successive frames and passes to the same plant. Selective spraying and harvesting require an agricultural robot to know which plant it is looking at. All of these depend on multi-object tracking (MOT) that assigns each physical object one identity, and keeps it, for as long as the object is in the video.

Agricultural video makes this hard in a specific way. Plants in a row, oranges on a tree and grape bunches on a cane are visually near-identical, so appearance embeddings learned for pedestrians separate them poorly. Leaves, branches and neighbouring fruit occlude them. Most importantly, the camera routinely leaves an object and comes back to it: a ground robot drives a row and returns along it, a handheld camera sweeps the canopy more than once, a UAV inspects a vine from several perspectives. Tracking-by-detection methods \citep{bewley2016sort,wojke2017deepsort,zhang2022bytetrack,aharon2022botsort,du2023strongsort} predict object motion in the image plane and keep an unmatched track alive for a fixed number of frames. An object that is out of view for longer is deleted and receives a new identity when it reappears. Every such switch inflates the count and fragments the plant's record (Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}).

The targets of agricultural tracking have a property that pedestrians and vehicles do not: they are stationary. A lettuce occupies the same place in the field on the outbound and return pass; an orange hangs at the same point of the canopy however often the camera sweeps past it. Its position in a three-dimensional map of the scene is therefore an identity cue that does not decay with time out of view and does not depend on appearance. Structure-from-motion (SfM) recovers such a map from the same monocular RGB video the tracker consumes \citep{schonberger2016colmap}.

A stationary object's map position is only useful for tracking if it can be compared with what the camera sees now. Comparing positions in 3D, as earlier fruit trackers do, is fragile: a landmark triangulated from a few keypoints is uncertain mostly along the viewing ray, which is exactly the direction that separates neighbouring fruit in depth. Projecting the landmark into the current image removes that direction, but needs the current camera. This paper presents MapSORT, which obtains that camera from the tracker itself. Every frame, the tracks that a standard frame-to-frame tracker has just matched supply correspondences between their landmarks and their boxes, and a robust resection recovers the frame's camera from them. With the camera, every stationary object, tracked, lost or long gone, has a predicted position in the current image. MapSORT uses it twice: as a motion model that re-matches tracks the frame-to-frame association missed, and as a re-identification cue that revives a track when its object comes back into view, however long it was away.

The contributions of this work are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A per-frame camera resection from the tracker's own landmark-to-box correspondences, robust to wrong matches and to near-coplanar scenes such as a field of lettuce, which places every stationary object, visible or not, in the current image.
\item MapSORT, which adds two stages to BoT-SORT \citep{aharon2022botsort}: a map-motion stage that re-matches unmatched tracks where the camera places them, and a revival stage that reclaims the identity of lost and dormant tracks by reprojection, including objects that re-enter at the frame edge with only a sliver visible. Tracks that leave the view are kept dormant instead of deleted, so identity survives absences of any length.
\item A revisit-centred evaluation protocol: a revisit identity preservation rate that decomposes CLEAR identity switches by the absence that preceded them, together with count error, which is the quantity yield estimation consumes.
\item A benchmark against StrongSORT, Deep OC-SORT and BoT-SORT on four public agricultural datasets (LettuceMOTS, MOrangeT, GrapeMOTS and AppleMOTS), covering four crops, four platforms and three viewing patterns, with per-sequence results, and a yield-count evaluation in a mango orchard against per-tree yield counts.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Tracking-by-detection}

SORT \citep{bewley2016sort} established the tracking-by-detection template: a constant-velocity Kalman filter \citep{kalman1960} predicts each track's box, and Hungarian matching \citep{kuhn1955hungarian} on intersection-over-union (IoU) assigns detections to tracks. DeepSORT \citep{wojke2017deepsort} added an appearance embedding and a matching cascade that prioritises recently seen tracks. ByteTrack \citep{zhang2022bytetrack} recovers low-confidence detections in a second association round; OC-SORT \citep{cao2023ocsort} corrects Kalman drift during occlusion from observations, and Deep OC-SORT \citep{maggiolino2023deepocsort} adds camera-motion compensation and an appearance cost whose weight adapts to how distinctive each detection's embedding is; FairMOT \citep{zhang2021fairmot} learns detection and embedding jointly. StrongSORT \citep{du2023strongsort} modernises DeepSORT with a stronger re-identification backbone and fused appearance and motion costs. BoT-SORT \citep{aharon2022botsort} compensates camera motion with a global image warp, refines the Kalman state, and fuses IoU and appearance costs; it is the base of MapSORT. SAM2MOT \citep{jiang2025sam2mot} departs from box association altogether: it uses the SAM~2 video segmentation model \citep{ravi2024sam2} to propagate object masks through time, so identity follows from segmentation rather than from matching.

All of these methods reason about identity in the image plane or in an appearance space. Neither is stable when the camera leaves an object and returns: image-plane motion over a long absence is not predictable, a global warp cannot follow the parallax of a three-dimensional canopy, and appearance galleries are bounded in both size and age. MapSORT keeps BoT-SORT's appearance and motion machinery and adds a cue that remains valid over any absence.

\subsection{Tracking and counting in crops and orchards}

Video-based fruit counting tracks fruit to avoid counting it more than once. \citet{halstead2018fruit} tracked sweet pepper with IoU association on a robotic platform. \citet{hani2020comparative} compared detection and counting pipelines for apple yield mapping, and \citet{villacres2023apple} compared tracking-by-detection algorithms for apple production estimation. \citet{hu2022lettucemot} released LettuceMOT with back-and-forth and out-and-in robot trajectories and showed that re-occurring plants defeat standard trackers; the same group introduced LettuceMOTS with a shape-feature MOTS method for precision spraying \citep{hu2024lettucemots}. Datasets for homogeneous-object MOTS exist for apples \citep{dejong2022applemots} and grape bunches captured from multiple UAV perspectives \citep{arizasentis2024grapemots}, following the MOTS task definition of \citet{voigtlaender2019mots}.

\subsection{Three-dimensional localisation for fruit identity}

Several works use multi-view geometry to localise fruit. \citet{stein2016mango} triangulated mango detections from a GNSS/INS-referenced vehicle to count fruit per tree. \citet{liu2019monocular} combined monocular SfM with semantic data association to count and map fruit. \citet{genemola2020sfm} projected instance segmentations onto an SfM point cloud to locate apples in 3D. \citet{rapado2023tomato} tracked tomatoes in 3D from a robot arm with known camera poses. \citet{santos2024moranget} relocalised oranges in 3D to recover them after occlusion and showed that removing relocalisation more than halves HOTA on MOrangeT.

These approaches either compare fruit positions in 3D with a fixed, hand-tuned distance threshold, or rely on camera poses from the reconstruction or an external sensor. MapSORT differs in three ways. Its landmarks are Gaussians whose uncertainty shrinks as observations are fused, and its 3D gate is a Mahalanobis distance that does not depend on the arbitrary scale of a monocular reconstruction. It recovers the camera that relates the map to the current image from its own tracks in every frame, so the projection it compares against is consistent with what the tracker currently believes. And it integrates the landmark cue into a modern tracker as a motion model and a revival stage, rather than replacing association.

\section{Materials and methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Problem formulation}\label{sec:problem}

The input is a monocular RGB video $\{I_1,\dots,I_T\}$ captured by a moving camera, with no depth, odometry or positioning sensor, and at each frame $t$ a set of detections $D_t=\{d_t^1,\dots,d_t^{n_t}\}$, each an axis-aligned box $b_t^i$ with centre $\bc_t^i$ and size $s_t^i$, the geometric mean of its width and height. The scene contains stationary objects. The task is to produce tracks $\mathcal{T}=\{\tau_1,\dots,\tau_K\}$, $\tau_k=\{(t,d_t^i)\}$, such that each track corresponds to exactly one physical object and each object to exactly one track: identity must be preserved through occlusion, visual ambiguity and camera motion, including when the camera returns to an object after it has been out of view.

\subsection{Overview}\label{sec:overview}

Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the MapSORT pipeline. A scene map is reconstructed once per sequence by SfM (Sect.~\ref{sec:map}), and every detection receives a landmark observation read from it (Sect.~\ref{sec:obs}). Tracks fuse their observations into a landmark (Sect.~\ref{sec:fusion}). At every frame, BoT-SORT's own association runs first. The pairs it matches, with the tracks' landmarks, yield the frame's camera (Sect.~\ref{sec:camera}). The camera then drives two further stages: tracks left unmatched are tried again where it places them, and lost and dormant tracks are revived where their landmarks project (Sect.~\ref{sec:assoc}). Tracks that leave the view are kept dormant rather than deleted (Sect.~\ref{sec:dormant}). Algorithm~\ref{alg:mapsort} summarises one frame.

\subsection{Scene map from structure-from-motion}\label{sec:map}

The scene map is a sparse SfM reconstruction of the sequence computed with COLMAP \citep{schonberger2016colmap}. SIFT features \citep{lowe2004sift} are extracted at full resolution, because the finest scale-space octaves carry the small objects, fruit and bunches, from which landmarks are read. All frames share one camera with a radial distortion model. Matching is exhaustive rather than sequential, so that frames from a revisit are matched directly to frames from the first pass and the two passes share 3D points; this is what places a returning object at the same map position. Incremental mapping with bundle adjustment then yields a point cloud and, for every registered frame, the 2D keypoints that observe each 3D point. When a sequence carries no camera metadata, a known focal length is held fixed during mapping and refined in a final bundle adjustment. The largest reconstructed model is kept. Its frame of reference and scale are arbitrary; MapSORT never needs metric scale.

\subsection{Landmark observations}\label{sec:obs}

For detection $d_t^i$ with box $b_t^i$, let $\mathcal{P}_t^i=\{\bX_j\}_{j=1}^{n}$ be the 3D points whose keypoints in frame $t$ fall inside $b_t^i$. Two optional filters, chosen per dataset, remove points that belong to the background rather than the object: an excess-green filter on point colour, $2g-r-b>\gamma$ on chromaticity, which separates foliage from soil, and a track-length filter that keeps points observed in at least $\ell_{\min}$ frames, which rejects poorly triangulated points. The landmark observation $z_t^i=(\bmu_t^i,\bSig_t^i,\bS_t^i)$ is
\begin{align}
\bmu_t^i &= \operatorname{median}(\mathcal{P}_t^i), \label{eq:mu}\\
\bS_t^i &= \operatorname{Cov}(\mathcal{P}_t^i) + r^2\bm{I}, \qquad r=\tfrac{1}{4}\,\lVert b_t^i\rVert\,\bar{z}/f, \label{eq:extent}\\
\bSig_t^i &= \bS_t^i / n, \label{eq:cov}
\end{align}
where the median is taken per coordinate, $\lVert b_t^i\rVert$ is the box diagonal in pixels, $\bar z$ the median camera-frame depth of the points and $f$ the focal length. The extent $\bS_t^i$ is the object's physical spread in the map frame; the isotropic floor $r^2\bm{I}$, the object's apparent radius back-projected to its depth, keeps it well conditioned when only a few points survive. The covariance $\bSig_t^i$ is the uncertainty about where the object's \emph{centre} lies, which shrinks as more points support the estimate. Only $\bSig_t^i$ is fused across observations; the extent is a property of the plant or fruit and does not shrink however often the object is observed. A detection whose box encloses no usable point carries no landmark, $z_t^i=\varnothing$, and takes part only in BoT-SORT's association.

\subsection{Landmark fusion}\label{sec:fusion}

Each track $k$ maintains its landmark in information form, accumulating the precision-weighted observations assigned to it:
\begin{equation}
\bLam_k=\sum_{(t,i)\in\tau_k}\big(\bSig_t^i\big)^{-1},\qquad
\bEta_k=\sum_{(t,i)\in\tau_k}\big(\bSig_t^i\big)^{-1}\bmu_t^i,\qquad
\hSig_k=\bLam_k^{-1},\quad \hmu_k=\bLam_k^{-1}\bEta_k. \label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}
This is the static-state information filter \citep{thrun2005probabilistic}: because the object does not move, there is no process noise, and the fused covariance $\hSig_k$ contracts with every observation. Where MapSORT compares landmarks in 3D, it uses the squared Mahalanobis distance between an observation and a fused landmark under their combined uncertainty,
\begin{equation}
d^2_{ki}=\big(\bmu_t^i-\hmu_k\big)^{\!\top}\big(\hSig_k+\bSig_t^i\big)^{-1}\big(\bmu_t^i-\hmu_k\big), \label{eq:maha}
\end{equation}
and forbids pairs with $d^2_{ki}>\tau$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:scale}
$d^2_{ki}$ is invariant to the similarity transform relating a monocular reconstruction to the metric scene.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Under $\bX\mapsto s\bm{R}\bX+\bm{t}$, every median, sample covariance, depth and back-projected radius in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:mu} to \eqref{eq:cov} transforms covariantly, so $\bmu\mapsto s\bm{R}\bmu+\bm{t}$ and $\bSig\mapsto s^2\bm{R}\bSig\bm{R}^{\top}$; Eq.~\eqref{eq:fusion} preserves this. The difference in Eq.~\eqref{eq:maha} scales by $s\bm{R}$ and the inverse covariance by $s^{-2}\bm{R}\,(\cdot)\,\bm{R}^\top$, which cancel.
\end{proof}

The gate $\tau$ is therefore a single dimensionless constant that transfers across sequences, crops and reconstructions, a property a fixed metric distance threshold lacks.

\subsection{Camera resection from tracked landmarks}\label{sec:camera}

A landmark built from a few keypoints is uncertain mostly along the viewing rays that triangulated it, which is the direction separating a fruit from its neighbours behind or in front of it. Projected into the current image, that error largely disappears. MapSORT therefore compares landmarks in the image, through a camera $\bP_t\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times4}$ fitted in every frame to the tracker's own state.

The correspondences are the pairs $(\hmu_k,\bc_t^i)$ of a track's fused landmark and a detection's box centre, taken from two sources: the track-detection pairs BoT-SORT has just matched (Sect.~\ref{sec:assoc}), and pairs of a track and a detection whose landmarks are mutual nearest neighbours under Eq.~\eqref{eq:maha} within the gate $\tau$. Errors are measured in box sizes, $e=\lVert\pi(\bP_t\hmu_k)-\bc_t^i\rVert/s_t^i$ with $\pi$ the perspective division, so one threshold serves small fruit and large plants alike. $\bP_t$ is estimated by RANSAC \citep{fischler1981ransac}, with the previous frame's camera competing against the random hypotheses and the winner refitted on its inliers for as long as that does not lose inliers. A camera is accepted when at least 8 correspondences agree with it to within half a box size.

Two camera models compete. The full projective camera is fitted by the direct linear transform from six correspondences \citep{hartley2004multiple}. It is ill-conditioned when the landmarks are close to coplanar, as on a field of lettuce seen from above, where any camera that agrees on the plane fits equally well. The second model fits the plane of the landmarks by principal component analysis and a homography $\bH$ from plane coordinates to the image, from four correspondences, lifted to $\bP_t=\bH\bm{F}$ with $\bm{F}$ the projection onto the plane. The plane model is preferred unless the full camera explains at least a tenth more correspondences. When no camera is accepted, MapSORT falls back to the Mahalanobis gate (Sect.~\ref{sec:assoc}).

Each track also learns where its landmark projects within its box. When a track is matched under an accepted camera, the residual $\bm{r}=(\bc_t^i-\pi(\bP_t\hmu_k))/s_t^i$ updates an exponential moving average $\bo_k$ (rate 0.3) of this offset, and a second average $\rho_k$ of $\lVert\bm{r}-\bo_k\rVert$ measures how steadily the camera places the track. A track is \emph{reliable} once it has two offsets and $\rho_k\le0.3$ box sizes. Its predicted box centre is $\pi(\bP_t\hmu_k)+s_k\bo_k$, with $s_k$ its size.

\subsection{Association}\label{sec:assoc}

Each frame runs three stages.

\paragraph{BoT-SORT association.} Tracks are predicted by BoT-SORT's Kalman filter after compensating global camera motion, and matched to detections on fused IoU and appearance costs \citep{aharon2022botsort}. These pairs, with the landmark pairs of Sect.~\ref{sec:camera}, yield the camera $\bP_t$.

\paragraph{Map motion.} Reliable tracks that the first stage left unmatched are moved to their predicted position under $\bP_t$, and the same costs are solved again between them and the detections still unmatched. This recovers tracks whose image-plane motion the Kalman filter could not follow, without disturbing any pair the first stage made. A track still unmatched keeps BoT-SORT's prediction.

\paragraph{Revival.} Detections still unmatched are offered to lost tracks, those missed for at most $A_{\max}$ frames, and to dormant tracks (Sect.~\ref{sec:dormant}), before any new identity is issued. Each candidate's box is predicted at its projected position and its last size not cut by the frame edge, and clipped to the frame, as the detector would see it. The revival cost is the distance between the centres of the clipped prediction and the detection, in box sizes of the candidate, and the assignment is solved by the Hungarian method with a gate of one box size. Candidates must have two offsets, so that their projection has been checked against their box. An object re-entering the view shows only a sliver at the frame edge, and the clipped predictions of several candidates crowd together there. A detection touching the frame edge is therefore only revived by a candidate whose clipped box overlaps it with an IoU of at least 0.3 and is at least half inside the frame. A new track born on such a detection, where a candidate is expected, stays unconfirmed and unreported for up to 8 frames, until enough of the object is visible to revive it or the wait runs out. When no camera is accepted, lost tracks are revived on the Mahalanobis distance of their latest observation and dormant tracks on that of their fused landmark.

Detections that remain unmatched start tentative tracks, which BoT-SORT confirms on a second match. Every matched or revived track fuses the detection's landmark observation by Eq.~\eqref{eq:fusion}.

\subsection{Dormant tracks and identity revival}\label{sec:dormant}

A frame-to-frame tracker deletes a track after $A_{\max}$ frames without a match, because beyond that horizon its image-plane prediction is no longer informative. For a stationary object, the landmark remains informative indefinitely. MapSORT therefore moves a confirmed track whose landmark has been observed to a dormant pool when it exceeds $A_{\max}$, instead of deleting it. A dormant track keeps its identity, fused landmark and offset. When the revival stage matches it to a detection, its Kalman state is re-initialised from the detection box, since the image-plane motion before the absence says nothing about where the object reappears, and its identity and landmark carry over unchanged. The dormant pool is unbounded, so an object keeps its identity across absences of any length.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{MapSORT update at frame $t$}\label{alg:mapsort}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require detections $D_t$ with embeddings $f_t^i$ and landmark observations $z_t^i$ (possibly $\varnothing$); active tracks $\mathcal{A}$ (tracked and lost); dormant pool $\mathcal{Z}$
\State Predict and motion-compensate every $k\in\mathcal{A}$; $(M_1,U_\mathcal{A},U_D)\gets$ BoT-SORT association
\State $\bP_t\gets$ RANSAC resection on $M_1$ and mutual-nearest landmark pairs within $\tau$
\If{$\bP_t$ accepted}
  \State $(M_2,U_\mathcal{A},U_D)\gets$ re-solve reliable $k\in U_\mathcal{A}$ at $\pi(\bP_t\hmu_k)+s_k\bo_k$ against $i\in U_D$
  \State $(M_3,U_D)\gets$ Hungarian on clipped reprojection error, lost $k\in U_\mathcal{A}$ and $k\in\mathcal{Z}$, $i\in U_D$, gate 1 box
\Else
  \State $(M_3,U_D)\gets$ Hungarian on $d^2_{ki}$, lost $k\in U_\mathcal{A}$ and $k\in\mathcal{Z}$, $i\in U_D$
\EndIf
\For{$(k,i)\in M_1\cup M_2\cup M_3$}
  \State Update $k$ with $d_t^i$ (re-initialise if revived); fuse $z_t^i$ into $(\bLam_k,\bEta_k)$
\EndFor
\State Learn offsets $\bo_k,\rho_k$ of matched tracks under $\bP_t$
\For{every $i\in U_D$}
  \State Match to a tentative track or start one; at the frame edge, near an expected revival, keep it unconfirmed
\EndFor
\State Move confirmed tracks lost for more than $A_{\max}$ frames with a landmark from $\mathcal{A}$ to $\mathcal{Z}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Datasets}\label{sec:datasets}

MapSORT is evaluated on four publicly available agricultural MOT datasets and on an orchard dataset collected for this work (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}, Fig.~\ref{fig:samples}). Together they cover five crops, four acquisition platforms and four viewing patterns: repeated passes along a row, multi-perspective inspection, orbits around a tree and continuous sweeps. Mask annotations are converted to axis-aligned boxes in MOT format. Per-sequence statistics are listed in Appendix~\ref{sec:app-seq}.

\begin{description}
\item[LettuceMOTS] \citep{hu2024lettucemots} was recorded by a downward-facing camera on a field robot over lettuce at three growth stages. The robot travels back and forth along the rows, so every plant is seen twice, with long absences in between. The three designated test sequences (0003, 0007, 0011) are used.
\item[MOrangeT] \citep{santos2024moranget} consists of smartphone videos, each sweeping one side of a sweet-orange tree in a commercial grove; the same fruit leaves and re-enters the field of view several times as the camera moves around the canopy. V01, V02 and V05 to V12 are used.
\item[GrapeMOTS] \citep{arizasentis2024grapemots} was recorded by a UAV over a vineyard. Its path-planned videos view the canopy from multiple perspectives to reduce leaf occlusion. PathPlanning\_1, PathPlanning\_2, PathPlanning\_3 and PathPlanning\_7 are used.

The test split (0006, 0007, 0008, 0010) is used; sequences 0011 and 0012 repeat 0007 and 0008 with locally edited pixels and are excluded. AppleMOTS sequences are continuous sweeps without revisits, and test MapSORT in short-term association among dense, near-identical fruit.

\subsection{Detections}\label{sec:detections}

Every tracker receives the same detections, produced by a YOLO detector \citep{jocher2023yolo} fine-tuned separately for each dataset. The detector is YOLO\tbd{} at an input size of \tbd{}\,px, initialised from COCO weights and fine-tuned on \tbd{}. Detections with a confidence above \tbd{} are passed to the trackers. On the evaluation sequences the detectors reach an AP$_{50}$ of \tbd{} on LettuceMOTS, \tbd{} on MOrangeT, \tbd{} on GrapeMOTS and \tbd{} on AppleMOTS.

\subsection{Baselines}\label{sec:baselines}

MapSORT is compared with three trackers that represent the main current approaches to tracking-by-detection. StrongSORT \citep{du2023strongsort} associates on appearance first, through a matching cascade over per-track feature galleries gated by Kalman motion, and compensates camera motion with ECC image alignment. Deep OC-SORT \citep{maggiolino2023deepocsort} extends observation-centric motion with sparse optical-flow camera-motion compensation and adaptively weighted appearance. BoT-SORT \citep{aharon2022botsort} fuses motion and appearance costs after camera-motion compensation. All three are run from BoxMOT \citep{brostrom2024boxmot} with their default configurations and with the same detections as MapSORT. The appearance-based trackers, MapSORT included, share one re-identification model per dataset: OSNet-x0.25 \citep{zhou2019osnet}, initialised from MSMT17 and fine-tuned on object identities from \tbd{}. BoT-SORT is also MapSORT's base tracker and shares all of its settings, so comparing the two isolates the effect of the camera and landmark stages.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}\label{sec:metrics}

Tracking accuracy is measured with HOTA and its detection and association components DetA and AssA \citep{luiten2021hota}, MOTA and identity switches (IDSW) from the CLEAR metrics \citep{bernardin2008clear}, and IDF1 \citep{ristani2016idf1}, all computed with TrackEval \citep{luiten2020trackeval} at an IoU threshold of 0.5 for CLEAR and Identity. Two further metrics address the requirements of agricultural use.

\paragraph{Count error.} Yield estimation from video counts tracks. The count error
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CE}=100\cdot\frac{|\mathrm{IDs}_{\text{pred}}|-|\mathrm{IDs}_{\text{GT}}|}{|\mathrm{IDs}_{\text{GT}}|}
\end{equation}
is the relative over-count (positive) or under-count (negative) of distinct identities; every identity switch that issues a new identity adds to it. On MangoYield, $|\mathrm{IDs}_{\text{pred}}|$ is the number of fruit identities a tracker issues over a tree's flight and $|\mathrm{IDs}_{\text{GT}}|$ is the tree's ground-truth yield count.

\paragraph{Revisit identity preservation.} A revisit event is a ground-truth object that reappears after at least $G=10$ consecutive frames out of view. At each event, the object's tracker identity is read, using exactly the per-frame assignment that CLEAR makes, at the first frame within a grace window of $W=5$ frames in which it is matched; the grace window allows for track confirmation after reappearance. The event is \emph{preserved} if that identity equals the one the object carried before the absence, a \emph{switch} if it differs, and \emph{unmatched} if the object is not matched within the window. The revisit identity preservation rate is
\begin{equation}
\RIP = 100\cdot\frac{\#\text{preserved}}{\#\text{preserved}+\#\text{switch}}.
\end{equation}
Because the per-frame assignment is CLEAR's own, every revisit switch is one of CLEAR's IDSW, so RIP measures the share of the hardest identity decisions, those after an absence, that a tracker gets right. Revisit outcomes are also reported as a function of absence length (Sect.~\ref{sec:res-revisit}).

\subsection{Identity persistence across revisits}\label{sec:res-revisit}

Fig.~\ref{fig:gap} breaks revisit outcomes down by how long the object was out of view. The baselines preserve identity only for short absences: 57.7\% (BoT-SORT), 79.6\% (StrongSORT) and 61.7\% (Deep OC-SORT) of events with a gap below 30 frames, and at most 3.1\% beyond, because a track lost for longer than its buffer is deleted. MapSORT preserves between 86.2\% and 94.0\% of events in every bin, and its rate does not fall with the length of the absence: it is 94.0\% for absences of 120 frames or more. This is the behaviour Sect.~\ref{sec:dormant} is designed to produce: the landmark of a stationary object does not age, so a dormant track is as recoverable after a long absence as after a short one.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The results show that, for stationary agricultural targets, a scene-map position is the identity cue that survives the conditions under which appearance and image-plane motion fail. The effect is largest exactly where agricultural tracking is hardest. When the camera returns to an object after a long absence, BoT-SORT's identity is lost by construction, and MapSORT recovers it at a rate that does not decay with the length of the absence (Fig.~\ref{fig:gap}). The datasets exercise this in different ways.

\paragraph{Repeated passes over a row.} LettuceMOTS is the operating pattern of a field robot working a row and returning, and the clearest case for the cue: on all three sequences MapSORT preserves at least 95\% of the returns, with 0, 3 and 2 identity switches against BoT-SORT's 29, 26 and 24, where no baseline makes fewer than 20, and an exact plant count on 0003. Two parts of the method matter here. The field is close to a plane, on which a full projective camera is ill-conditioned; fitting the plane's homography instead keeps the camera stable. And a returning plant enters the image at its edge, where the detector sees only a sliver of it for the first frames; comparing clipped predictions and holding a new track at the edge until the plant can be revived is what lets it reclaim its identity before a new one is issued. The ablation also shows that the dense map support of a lettuce field makes the 3D landmark gate alone very effective there, more than the camera-based revival; MapSORT uses the 3D gate only as a fallback, in favour of a single configuration that also serves the sparse orange landmarks.

\paragraph{Sweeps around a canopy.} MOrangeT is the largest and most revisit-heavy dataset, with 950 revisits over ten sequences. A handheld sweep moves the camera close to a three-dimensional canopy, so image-plane motion is dominated by parallax that a global camera-motion warp cannot follow, and fruit go out of view behind leaves and the trunk for hundreds of frames. MapSORT preserves 92.0\% of these revisits and has fewer identity switches than every baseline on every sequence, 86 in total against 829 for the best baseline, Deep OC-SORT. Its landmarks there rest on a median of two map points, so a 3D comparison is unreliable; projecting through a camera fitted to many landmarks at once is what makes them usable.

\paragraph{Multi-perspective UAV inspection.} On GrapeMOTS the landmarks of the bunches along a vine lie close to a line, which constrains the camera poorly across it. MapSORT still preserves 78.3\% of the revisits of PathPlanning\_7, of which the best baseline preserves 4.3\%, and makes no more identity switches than any baseline on every sequence. On PathPlanning\_3, however, its HOTA and IDF1 are below those of all three baselines. On this sequence the ground truth gives bunches that are seen again after a motion-blurred pass new identities, so the sequence contains no revisit events, while MapSORT links the two views of the same bunch. In ground-truth terms each such link merges two objects, which the identity metrics penalise without counting an identity switch. Annotation policies for when an object seen again counts as the same object are not uniform across datasets, and a tracker built to recover identity across absences is, by design, at odds with a policy that resets it.

\paragraph{Dense fruit without revisits.} AppleMOTS has no revisits, and its fruit are small, numerous and alike, so it tests whether the extra stages hurt short-term association. They do not: MapSORT has the highest HOTA on three of four sequences, and fewer identity switches than every baseline on three. On AppleMOTS 0007 it is 0.4 HOTA below BoT-SORT: a few lost tracks are revived onto newly appearing apples next to where their own apple was, because the camera fitted to small, densely packed fruit places them with errors of a third of a box, comparable to the spacing of the fruit, and appearance does not separate apples. The single identity switch on AppleMOTS 0008, which BoT-SORT also makes, is an apple without a landmark whose motion differs from its neighbours' by parallax, which neither BoT-SORT's image-plane motion nor a landmark resolves; Deep OC-SORT avoids it, at 2.2 points lower HOTA.

\paragraph{Consequences for precision agriculture.} The most direct consequence is counting. Every identity switch that issues a new identity is a phantom plant or fruit in the count. The baselines over-count by 43\% to 123\% on the representative sequences with revisits, which would make their output unusable as a yield estimate without heavy post-hoc correction; MapSORT's count error there is between 0\% and 8\%. A second consequence is per-plant records. Because a plant keeps its identity across passes, measurements taken on the outbound and return passes, or from different UAV perspectives, attach to the same plant, which is what plant-level phenotyping and selective treatment need. A third is spatial: every MapSORT track carries a position in the scene map, so a track list is also a plant map. Registering the map to a GNSS-referenced frame, as is routine in orchard and field mapping, would turn MapSORT's output into a georeferenced inventory of individual plants or fruit. MapSORT also composes with an existing tracker rather than replacing it: BoT-SORT's association is unchanged and runs first, and the camera stages act only on what it leaves unmatched, so improvements in detectors, appearance models or the base tracker carry over directly.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The camera and revival stages cost throughput: on a CPU, MapSORT runs at 29\% to 56\% of BoT-SORT's frame rate. Their settings are shared across all datasets, but were chosen on the same public sequences on which they are evaluated, since these datasets provide no separate validation split; the MangoYield evaluation tests them on data that played no part in their design. Detections without a landmark, such as small fruit at the limit of the map's resolution, benefit only from BoT-SORT's own association.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

MapSORT turns the stationarity of crops and fruit into an identity cue. It gives every detection and track a Gaussian landmark in a structure-from-motion map of the scene, fits a camera to its own tracks in every frame, and uses that camera to re-match the tracks its base tracker misses and to revive tracks, kept dormant rather than deleted, when their objects return, including at the edge of the image. On four public agricultural datasets covering lettuce, oranges, grapes and apples, recorded from a field robot, a handheld camera, a wearable camera and a UAV, MapSORT outperforms StrongSORT, Deep OC-SORT and BoT-SORT, has fewer identity switches than every baseline on 19 of the 21 test sequences, preserves identity across revisits of any length, and produces substantially more accurate counts. MapSORT currently builds its scene map offline. Recent online reconstruction models that recover scene geometry frame by frame from monocular video \citep{wang2025cut3r,murai2025mast3rslam} are a natural drop-in source for the same landmark observations, and extending MapSORT to online operation on a field robot is the subject of future work.
