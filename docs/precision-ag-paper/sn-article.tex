\title[MapSORT]{MapSORT: Landmark Association for Identity-Persistent Tracking of Stationary Crops and Fruit}

\author*{\fnm{Srihari} \sur{Vemuru}}\email{vemshari27@gmail.com}

\abstract{\textbf{Purpose:} Plant- and fruit-level records in precision agriculture, such as yield estimates, phenotyping logs and targeted spraying, require every plant or fruit to keep one identity across a video, including when the camera leaves and later returns to it. General-purpose multi-object trackers lose identity in exactly this situation, because crops look alike and an object out of view for more than a few frames is forgotten. This work turns the property that sets agricultural targets apart, that they do not move, into an association cue that keeps identities intact across occlusions and revisits.

\textbf{Methods:} MapSORT reconstructs the scene with structure-from-motion and gives every detection and every track a landmark: a Gaussian over the object's position in the scene map. Built on BoT-SORT, it fits a camera to its own tracks in every frame, from their landmarks and boxes, and uses that camera to place each stationary object in the image. Tracks the frame-to-frame association misses are re-matched where their landmark projects; lost tracks, and tracks that left the view long ago and were kept dormant rather than deleted, are revived the same way, including objects that re-enter at the frame edge. MapSORT is compared with StrongSORT, Deep OC-SORT and BoT-SORT on four public datasets (LettuceMOTS, MOrangeT, GrapeMOTS, AppleMOTS).

\textbf{Results:} Averaged over the four datasets, MapSORT reaches 95.5 HOTA and 96.0 IDF1, 10.2 and 16.7 points above the strongest baseline, BoT-SORT, and cuts identity switches from 1150, the fewest of any baseline, to 223. It preserves identity in 91.9\% of 1043 revisit events, against at most 11.5\% for the baselines, at every length of absence. It has fewer identity switches than every baseline on 19 of the 21 test sequences.

\textbf{Conclusion:} The position of a stationary object in a map of the scene is an identity cue that survives arbitrarily long absences. Recovering the camera from the tracks themselves turns that position into a motion model and a re-identification cue, giving identity-consistent tracks and accurate counts across crops, platforms and viewing patterns.}

\keywords{Multi-object tracking, Structure-from-motion, Camera resection, Fruit counting, Plant re-identification, Yield estimation}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

% ---------------- Beat 1: the need ----------------
Precision agriculture is moving from field-level statistics to operations on individual plants and fruit.
Selective harvesting robots pick only the ripe fruit and leave the rest \citep{rajendran2024selective};
targeted sprayers apply agrochemicals where sensing detects a target instead of covering the whole orchard \citep{mahmud2021precision,wu2025orchard};
and plant-level management and phenotyping aim to sense, and treat, plants individually rather than by field, and to measure their fine-scale traits \citep{sarkar2024cyber,xu2022phenotyping}.

Each of these operations needs a plant or fruit to keep one identity across the whole video.
The fruit a robot has chosen to pick must still be that fruit when the arm reaches it; the treatments and measurements a plant receives must accumulate on a single record; and a fruit seen again from another side must not become a second fruit.
Identity must hold when the camera leaves an object and later returns to it, as on the return pass of a field robot along a row, in a UAV flight that views a vineyard from several perspectives, or in a handheld sweep around a tree.
When identity breaks, the consequence is physical rather than statistical: a fruit is picked or treated twice, or skipped.
Counting is the coarsest of these tasks, since every identity switch adds a phantom plant to the total.

% ---------------- Beat 2: current SOTA and their gap ----------------
The state of the art for tracking plants and fruit in video is tracking-by-detection.
StrongSORT \citep{du2023strongsort}, Deep OC-SORT \citep{maggiolino2023deepocsort} and BoT-SORT \citep{aharon2022botsort} detect objects in every frame and associate the detections with existing tracks mainly through appearance: a re-identification embedding of each detection is compared with those of the tracks, while image-plane motion, through a Kalman-filter prediction and camera-motion compensation, supports and gates the match.
This works when targets are visually distinct and mostly stay in view, as in the pedestrian tracking these trackers were developed for.

These trackers fail to keep one identity per plant or fruit once the camera leaves an object and returns to it, and to tell apart neighbouring objects of the same crop.
There are three reasons.
First, plants and fruit of one crop look almost alike, so appearance separates them poorly, and what does differ, such as viewpoint, occlusion and lighting, changes between passes.
Second, motion cues are tied to the image plane and to recent frames; camera motion, and in a canopy the parallax it produces, breaks them.
Third, a track is deleted once it has been lost for longer than a buffer, because beyond that horizon its image-plane prediction is no longer informative; an object that stays out of view for longer is forgotten by construction and receives a new identity when it returns.
Stronger appearance models and better motion compensation improve short-term association, but not this.
Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser} shows the result: on the return pass of a field robot along two lettuce rows (LettuceMOTS; \citealt{hu2024lettucemots}), BoT-SORT has issued 56 identities for 37 plants, and every plant in view carries a new one.
Each such switch is a phantom plant or fruit, which defeats any task that must act on a specific one.

% ---------------- Beat 3: insight and overview ----------------
Agricultural targets do not move, and this is the property that sets them apart from pedestrians.
A stationary object's position in a map of the scene does not age: unlike appearance and image-plane motion, it is as valid after a long absence as after a short one.
Position can therefore serve as an identity memory that outlasts the track buffer, which is exactly what the failures above lack.

MapSORT builds this memory on top of BoT-SORT \citep{aharon2022botsort}, whose own association runs first and is left unchanged.
A scene map is reconstructed once per sequence by structure-from-motion \citep{schonberger2016colmap}, and every detection and track receives a Gaussian landmark, a position in the map together with its uncertainty.
At every frame, a camera is fitted to the tracker's own landmark and box correspondences, with a plane model for near-planar scenes such as a lettuce field, and this camera predicts where each stationary object should appear in the image, including just beyond the frame edge.
Two stages use it: a map-motion stage re-matches the tracks that BoT-SORT left unmatched where the camera places them, and a revival stage reclaims the identity of lost tracks by reprojection.
Tracks that leave the view are kept dormant rather than deleted, so an object can reclaim its identity after an absence of any length.
Because every track also carries a position in the scene map, a track list is at the same time a map of the plants, which is what an operation on a specific plant needs in addition to an identity.

% ---------------- Contributions ----------------
The contributions of this work are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A Gaussian landmark for every detection and track, compared through a Mahalanobis gate that is invariant to the unknown scale of a monocular reconstruction (Proposition~\ref{prop:scale}), and a per-frame camera resection from the tracker's own landmark-to-box correspondences, robust to wrong matches and to near-coplanar scenes such as a field of lettuce, which places every stationary object, visible or not, in the current image.
  \item MapSORT, which adds two stages to BoT-SORT \citep{aharon2022botsort}: a map-motion stage that re-matches unmatched tracks where the camera places them, and a revival stage that reclaims the identity of lost and dormant tracks by reprojection, including objects that re-enter at the frame edge with only a sliver visible. Tracks that leave the view are kept dormant instead of deleted, so identity survives absences of any length.
  \item A revisit-centred evaluation protocol: a revisit identity preservation rate that decomposes CLEAR identity switches \citep{bernardin2008clear} by the absence that preceded them, together with count error, the quantity that yield estimation consumes.
  \item A benchmark against StrongSORT \citep{du2023strongsort}, Deep OC-SORT \citep{maggiolino2023deepocsort} and BoT-SORT \citep{aharon2022botsort} on four public agricultural datasets, LettuceMOTS \citep{hu2024lettucemots}, MOrangeT \citep{santos2024moranget}, GrapeMOTS \citep{arizasentis2024grapemots} and AppleMOTS \citep{dejong2022applemots}, covering four crops, four platforms and three viewing patterns, with per-sequence results.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

% ---------------- Paragraph 1: tracking-by-detection in agriculture ----------------
Counting and tracking fruit in video is commonly done by tracking-by-detection: a detector finds the fruit in every frame, a tracker links the detections into identities, and the identities are then counted.
OrangeSORT builds on DeepSORT for citrus counting \citep{zhang2022citrus}, \citet{wang2019mango} pair a mango detector with a Kalman filter and Hungarian assignment, and \citet{osman2021yield} estimate apple yield from video with a modified DeepSORT.
Public datasets with per-object identity annotations now allow tracking itself to be evaluated, not only the final count: LettuceMOTS \citep{hu2024lettucemots}, MOrangeT \citep{santos2024moranget}, GrapeMOTS \citep{arizasentis2024grapemots} and AppleMOTS \citep{dejong2022applemots}.
Trackers built on promptable segmentation foundation models have also recently appeared, such as SAM2MOT \citep{jiang2026sam2mot}, which places segmentation at the centre of tracking and runs zero-shot.

% ---------------- Paragraph 2: position as an association cue (existence only) ----------------
Position has also been used as a cue for stationary produce.
\citet{santos2024moranget} relocalise fruit in 3D to handle occluded and re-appearing oranges, \citet{liu2019monocular} combine monocular structure-from-motion with semantic data association to map fruit in 3D, though only to count them, without evaluating tracking, and LocalizeSORT \citep{vemuru2024localizesort}, a preprint, adds a world-coordinate term to the association cost.

% ---------------- Paragraph 3: the SORT lineage ----------------
The trackers that this work builds on and compares with form one lineage.
SORT \citep{bewley2016sort} predicts each track with a Kalman filter and assigns detections to tracks by the Hungarian method on box overlap, and DeepSORT \citep{wojke2017deepsort} adds a learned appearance embedding to the association cost.
StrongSORT \citep{du2023strongsort} revisits DeepSORT with a stronger appearance model, camera-motion compensation by image alignment and an adaptive Kalman filter.
Deep OC-SORT \citep{maggiolino2023deepocsort} adds observation-centric motion estimation, camera-motion compensation by sparse optical flow and adaptively weighted appearance.
BoT-SORT \citep{aharon2022botsort} fuses motion and appearance costs after camera-motion compensation, and is the base tracker of MapSORT (Section~\ref{sec:methods}).
Despite their differences, all of them associate detections with tracks over a local window of recent frames, and all delete a track once it has been lost for a fixed number of frames.

\section{Materials and methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Problem formulation}\label{sec:problem}

The input is a monocular RGB video $\{I_1,\dots,I_T\}$ captured by a moving camera, with no depth, odometry or positioning sensor, and at each frame $t$ a set of detections $D_t=\{d_t^1,\dots,d_t^{n_t}\}$, each an axis-aligned box $b_t^i$ with centre $\bc_t^i$ and size $s_t^i$, the geometric mean of its width and height. The scene contains stationary objects. The task is to produce tracks $\mathcal{T}=\{\tau_1,\dots,\tau_K\}$, $\tau_k=\{(t,d_t^i)\}$, such that each track corresponds to exactly one physical object and each object to exactly one track: identity must be preserved through occlusion, visual ambiguity and camera motion, including when the camera returns to an object after it has been out of view.

\subsection{Overview}\label{sec:overview}

Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the MapSORT pipeline. A scene map is reconstructed once per sequence by SfM (Sect.~\ref{sec:map}), and every detection receives a landmark observation read from it (Sect.~\ref{sec:obs}). Tracks fuse their observations into a landmark (Sect.~\ref{sec:fusion}). At every frame, BoT-SORT's own association runs first. The pairs it matches, with the tracks' landmarks, yield the frame's camera (Sect.~\ref{sec:camera}). The camera then drives two further stages: tracks left unmatched are tried again where it places them, and lost and dormant tracks are revived where their landmarks project (Sect.~\ref{sec:assoc}). Tracks that leave the view are kept dormant rather than deleted (Sect.~\ref{sec:dormant}). Algorithm~\ref{alg:mapsort} summarises one frame.

\subsection{Scene map from structure-from-motion}\label{sec:map}

The scene map is a sparse SfM reconstruction of the sequence computed with COLMAP \citep{schonberger2016colmap}. SIFT features \citep{lowe2004sift} are extracted at full resolution, because the finest scale-space octaves carry the small objects, fruit and bunches, from which landmarks are read. All frames share one camera with a radial distortion model. Matching is exhaustive rather than sequential, so that frames from a revisit are matched directly to frames from the first pass and the two passes share 3D points; this is what places a returning object at the same map position. The two 1080p GrapeMOTS sequences, which have no revisits and up to 927 frames, are instead matched sequentially, each frame against its 30 neighbours. Incremental mapping with bundle adjustment then yields a point cloud and, for every registered frame, the 2D keypoints that observe each 3D point. The frames carry no camera metadata. One sequence per camera is therefore mapped with free intrinsics; where that fragments the model, it is mapped again with the focal length of its largest fragment held fixed. The resulting camera is held fixed while the other sequences from the same camera are mapped, and refined in a final bundle adjustment. The largest reconstructed model is kept. MOrangeT ships a COLMAP model of every sequence with all frames registered \citep{santos2024moranget}, which is used as it is. The frame of reference and scale of a model are arbitrary; MapSORT never needs metric scale.

\subsection{Landmark observations}\label{sec:obs}

For detection $d_t^i$ with box $b_t^i$, let $\mathcal{P}_t^i=\{\bX_j\}_{j=1}^{n}$ be the 3D points whose keypoints in frame $t$ fall inside $b_t^i$. Three optional filters, chosen per sequence, remove points that belong to the background rather than the object or are poorly triangulated. A mask filter keeps only points whose keypoint falls on the detection's instance mask, optionally eroded by a few pixels where the mask boundary bleeds into the background; when no point falls on the mask, the points in the box are kept. A track-length filter keeps points observed in at least $\ell_{\min}$ frames. A depth filter drops points more than $k$ median absolute deviations deeper than the median camera-frame depth of the box's points, which removes background seen through gaps in the canopy. The landmark observation $z_t^i=(\bmu_t^i,\bSig_t^i,\bS_t^i)$ is
\begin{align}
\bmu_t^i &= \operatorname{median}(\mathcal{P}_t^i), \label{eq:mu}\\
\bS_t^i &= \operatorname{Cov}(\mathcal{P}_t^i) + r^2\bm{I}, \qquad r=\tfrac{1}{4}\,\lVert b_t^i\rVert\,\bar{z}/f, \label{eq:extent}\\
\bSig_t^i &= \bS_t^i / n, \label{eq:cov}
\end{align}
where the median is taken per coordinate, $\lVert b_t^i\rVert$ is the box diagonal in pixels, $\bar z$ the median camera-frame depth of the points and $f$ the focal length. The extent $\bS_t^i$ is the object's physical spread in the map frame; the isotropic floor $r^2\bm{I}$, the object's apparent radius back-projected to its depth, keeps it well conditioned when only a few points survive. The covariance $\bSig_t^i$ is the uncertainty about where the object's \emph{centre} lies, which shrinks as more points support the estimate. Only $\bSig_t^i$ is fused across observations; the extent is a property of the plant or fruit and does not shrink however often the object is observed. A detection whose box encloses no usable point carries no landmark, $z_t^i=\varnothing$, and takes part only in BoT-SORT's association.

\subsection{Landmark fusion}\label{sec:fusion}

Each track $k$ maintains its landmark in information form, accumulating the precision-weighted observations assigned to it:
\begin{equation}
\bLam_k=\sum_{(t,i)\in\tau_k}\big(\bSig_t^i\big)^{-1},\qquad
\bEta_k=\sum_{(t,i)\in\tau_k}\big(\bSig_t^i\big)^{-1}\bmu_t^i,\qquad
\hSig_k=\bLam_k^{-1},\quad \hmu_k=\bLam_k^{-1}\bEta_k. \label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}
This is the static-state information filter \citep{thrun2005probabilistic}: because the object does not move, there is no process noise, and the fused covariance $\hSig_k$ contracts with every observation. Where MapSORT compares landmarks in 3D, it uses the squared Mahalanobis distance between an observation and a fused landmark under their combined uncertainty,
\begin{equation}
d^2_{ki}=\big(\bmu_t^i-\hmu_k\big)^{\!\top}\big(\hSig_k+\bSig_t^i\big)^{-1}\big(\bmu_t^i-\hmu_k\big), \label{eq:maha}
\end{equation}
and forbids pairs with $d^2_{ki}>\tau$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:scale}
$d^2_{ki}$ is invariant to the similarity transform relating a monocular reconstruction to the metric scene.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Under $\bX\mapsto s\bm{R}\bX+\bm{t}$, every median, sample covariance, depth and back-projected radius in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:mu} to \eqref{eq:cov} transforms covariantly, so $\bmu\mapsto s\bm{R}\bmu+\bm{t}$ and $\bSig\mapsto s^2\bm{R}\bSig\bm{R}^{\top}$; Eq.~\eqref{eq:fusion} preserves this. The difference in Eq.~\eqref{eq:maha} scales by $s\bm{R}$ and the inverse covariance by $s^{-2}\bm{R}\,(\cdot)\,\bm{R}^\top$, which cancel.
\end{proof}

The gate $\tau$ is therefore a single dimensionless constant that transfers across sequences, crops and reconstructions, a property a fixed metric distance threshold lacks.

\subsection{Camera resection from tracked landmarks}\label{sec:camera}

A landmark built from a few keypoints is uncertain mostly along the viewing rays that triangulated it, which is the direction separating a fruit from its neighbours behind or in front of it. Projected into the current image, that error largely disappears. MapSORT therefore compares landmarks in the image, through a camera $\bP_t\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times4}$ fitted in every frame to the tracker's own state.

The correspondences are the pairs $(\hmu_k,\bc_t^i)$ of a track's fused landmark and a detection's box centre, taken from two sources: the track-detection pairs BoT-SORT has just matched (Sect.~\ref{sec:assoc}), and pairs of a track and a detection whose landmarks are mutual nearest neighbours under Eq.~\eqref{eq:maha} within the gate $\tau$. Errors are measured in box sizes, $e=\lVert\pi(\bP_t\hmu_k)-\bc_t^i\rVert/s_t^i$ with $\pi$ the perspective division, so one threshold serves small fruit and large plants alike. $\bP_t$ is estimated by RANSAC \citep{fischler1981ransac}, with the previous frame's camera competing against the random hypotheses and the winner refitted on its inliers for as long as that does not lose inliers. A camera is accepted when at least 8 correspondences agree with it to within half a box size.

Two camera models compete. The full projective camera is fitted by the direct linear transform from six correspondences \citep{hartley2004multiple}. It is ill-conditioned when the landmarks are close to coplanar, as on a field of lettuce seen from above, where any camera that agrees on the plane fits equally well. The second model fits the plane of the landmarks by principal component analysis and a homography $\bH$ from plane coordinates to the image, from four correspondences, lifted to $\bP_t=\bH\bm{F}$ with $\bm{F}$ the projection onto the plane. The plane model is preferred unless the full camera explains at least a tenth more correspondences. When no camera is accepted, MapSORT falls back to the Mahalanobis gate (Sect.~\ref{sec:assoc}).

Each track also learns where its landmark projects within its box. When a track is matched under an accepted camera, the residual $\bm{r}=(\bc_t^i-\pi(\bP_t\hmu_k))/s_t^i$ updates an exponential moving average $\bo_k$ (rate 0.3) of this offset, and a second average $\rho_k$ of $\lVert\bm{r}-\bo_k\rVert$ measures how steadily the camera places the track. A track is \emph{reliable} once it has two offsets and $\rho_k\le0.3$ box sizes. Its predicted box centre is $\pi(\bP_t\hmu_k)+s_k\bo_k$, with $s_k$ its size.

\subsection{Association}\label{sec:assoc}

Each frame runs three stages.

\paragraph{BoT-SORT association.} Tracks are predicted by BoT-SORT's Kalman filter after compensating global camera motion, and matched to detections on fused IoU and appearance costs \citep{aharon2022botsort}. These pairs, with the landmark pairs of Sect.~\ref{sec:camera}, yield the camera $\bP_t$.

\paragraph{Map motion.} Reliable tracks that the first stage left unmatched are moved to their predicted position under $\bP_t$, and the same costs are solved again between them and the detections still unmatched. This recovers tracks whose image-plane motion the Kalman filter could not follow, without disturbing any pair the first stage made. A track still unmatched keeps BoT-SORT's prediction.

\paragraph{Revival.} Detections still unmatched are offered to lost tracks, those missed for at most $A_{\max}$ frames, and to dormant tracks (Sect.~\ref{sec:dormant}), before any new identity is issued. Each candidate's box is predicted at its projected position and its last size not cut by the frame edge, and clipped to the frame, as the detector would see it. The revival cost is the distance between the centres of the clipped prediction and the detection, in box sizes of the candidate, and the assignment is solved by the Hungarian method with a gate of one box size. Candidates must have two offsets, so that their projection has been checked against their box. An object re-entering the view shows only a sliver at the frame edge, and the clipped predictions of several candidates crowd together there. A detection touching the frame edge is therefore only revived by a candidate whose clipped box overlaps it with an IoU of at least 0.3 and is at least half inside the frame. A new track born on such a detection, where a candidate is expected, stays unconfirmed and unreported for up to 8 frames, until enough of the object is visible to revive it or the wait runs out. When no camera is accepted, lost tracks are revived on the Mahalanobis distance of their latest observation and dormant tracks on that of their fused landmark.

Detections that remain unmatched start tentative tracks, which BoT-SORT confirms on a second match. Every matched or revived track fuses the detection's landmark observation by Eq.~\eqref{eq:fusion}.

\subsection{Dormant tracks and identity revival}\label{sec:dormant}

A frame-to-frame tracker deletes a track after $A_{\max}$ frames without a match, because beyond that horizon its image-plane prediction is no longer informative. For a stationary object, the landmark remains informative indefinitely. MapSORT therefore moves a confirmed track whose landmark has been observed to a dormant pool when it exceeds $A_{\max}$, instead of deleting it. A dormant track keeps its identity, fused landmark and offset. When the revival stage matches it to a detection, its Kalman state is re-initialised from the detection box, since the image-plane motion before the absence says nothing about where the object reappears, and its identity and landmark carry over unchanged. The dormant pool is unbounded, so an object keeps its identity across absences of any length.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{MapSORT update at frame $t$}\label{alg:mapsort}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require detections $D_t$ with embeddings $f_t^i$ and landmark observations $z_t^i$ (possibly $\varnothing$); active tracks $\mathcal{A}$ (tracked and lost); dormant pool $\mathcal{Z}$
\State Predict and motion-compensate every $k\in\mathcal{A}$; $(M_1,U_\mathcal{A},U_D)\gets$ BoT-SORT association
\State $\bP_t\gets$ RANSAC resection on $M_1$ and mutual-nearest landmark pairs within $\tau$
\If{$\bP_t$ accepted}
  \State $(M_2,U_\mathcal{A},U_D)\gets$ re-solve reliable $k\in U_\mathcal{A}$ at $\pi(\bP_t\hmu_k)+s_k\bo_k$ against $i\in U_D$
  \State $(M_3,U_D)\gets$ Hungarian on clipped reprojection error, lost $k\in U_\mathcal{A}$ and $k\in\mathcal{Z}$, $i\in U_D$, gate 1 box
\Else
  \State $(M_3,U_D)\gets$ Hungarian on $d^2_{ki}$, lost $k\in U_\mathcal{A}$ and $k\in\mathcal{Z}$, $i\in U_D$
\EndIf
\For{$(k,i)\in M_1\cup M_2\cup M_3$}
  \State Update $k$ with $d_t^i$ (re-initialise if revived); fuse $z_t^i$ into $(\bLam_k,\bEta_k)$
\EndFor
\State Learn offsets $\bo_k,\rho_k$ of matched tracks under $\bP_t$
\For{every $i\in U_D$}
  \State Match to a tentative track or start one; at the frame edge, near an expected revival, keep it unconfirmed
\EndFor
\State Move confirmed tracks lost for more than $A_{\max}$ frames with a landmark from $\mathcal{A}$ to $\mathcal{Z}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Datasets}\label{sec:datasets}

MapSORT is evaluated on four publicly available agricultural MOT datasets (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}, Fig.~\ref{fig:samples}). Together they cover four crops, four acquisition platforms and three viewing patterns: repeated passes along a row, multi-perspective inspection and continuous sweeps. Mask annotations are converted to axis-aligned boxes in MOT format. Per-sequence statistics are listed in Appendix~\ref{sec:app-seq}.

\begin{description}
\item[LettuceMOTS] \citep{hu2024lettucemots} was recorded by a downward-facing camera on a field robot over lettuce at three growth stages. The robot travels back and forth along the rows, so every plant is seen twice, with long absences in between. The three designated test sequences (0003, 0007, 0011) are used.
\item[MOrangeT] \citep{santos2024moranget} consists of smartphone videos, each sweeping one side of a sweet-orange tree in a commercial grove; the same fruit leaves and re-enters the field of view several times as the camera moves around the canopy. V01, V02 and V05 to V12 are used.
\item[GrapeMOTS] \citep{arizasentis2024grapemots} was recorded by a UAV over a vineyard. Its path-planned videos view the canopy from multiple perspectives to reduce leaf occlusion. PathPlanning\_1, PathPlanning\_2, PathPlanning\_3 and PathPlanning\_7 are used.
\item[AppleMOTS] \citep{dejong2022applemots} contains UAV and wearable-camera video of apple trees with dense clusters of small, similar fruit. The test split (0006, 0007, 0008, 0010) is used; sequences 0011 and 0012 repeat 0007 and 0008 with locally edited pixels and are excluded. AppleMOTS sequences are continuous sweeps without revisits, and test MapSORT in short-term association among dense, near-identical fruit.
\end{description}

\subsection{Detections}\label{sec:detections}

Every tracker receives the same detections, produced by a YOLO detector \citep{jocher2023yolo} fine-tuned separately for each dataset. On the three MOTS datasets it is the instance-segmentation variant, whose masks the landmark filter of Sect.~\ref{sec:obs} uses. The detector is YOLOv8m (YOLOv8m-seg on the MOTS datasets) at an input size of 1280\,px, initialised from COCO weights and fine-tuned on the same sequences outside the test data that train the re-identification model (Sect.~\ref{sec:baselines}). Detections with a confidence above 0.1 are passed to the trackers. On the evaluation sequences the detectors reach an AP$_{50}$ of 96.2 on LettuceMOTS, 87.4 on MOrangeT, 89.1 on GrapeMOTS and 81.3 on AppleMOTS.

\subsection{Baselines}\label{sec:baselines}

MapSORT is compared with three trackers that represent the main current approaches to tracking-by-detection. StrongSORT \citep{du2023strongsort} associates on appearance first, through a matching cascade over per-track feature galleries gated by Kalman motion, and compensates camera motion with ECC image alignment. Deep OC-SORT \citep{maggiolino2023deepocsort} extends observation-centric motion with sparse optical-flow camera-motion compensation and adaptively weighted appearance. BoT-SORT \citep{aharon2022botsort} fuses motion and appearance costs after camera-motion compensation. All three are run from BoxMOT \citep{brostrom2024boxmot} with their default configurations and with the same detections as MapSORT. The appearance-based trackers, MapSORT included, share one re-identification model per dataset: OSNet-x0.25 \citep{zhou2019osnet}, initialised from MSMT17 and fine-tuned on ground-truth object identities from sequences outside the test data: AppleMOTS 0000 to 0004, GrapeMOTS PathPlanning\_4 to \_6, LettuceMOTS 0000, 0004 and 0008, and MOrangeT V03. One further sequence per dataset (AppleMOTS 0005, GrapeMOTS PathPlanning\_8, LettuceMOTS 0009, MOrangeT V04) selects the training epoch. Each dataset contributes at most 200 identities with up to 10 crops each, spread evenly along each track, and the model is trained for 10 epochs with label-smoothed cross-entropy and a batch-hard triplet loss. BoT-SORT is also MapSORT's base tracker and shares all of its settings, so comparing the two isolates the effect of the camera and landmark stages.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}\label{sec:metrics}

Tracking accuracy is measured with HOTA and its detection and association components DetA and AssA \citep{luiten2021hota}, MOTA and identity switches (IDSW) from the CLEAR metrics \citep{bernardin2008clear}, and IDF1 \citep{ristani2016idf1}, all computed with TrackEval \citep{luiten2020trackeval} at an IoU threshold of 0.5 for CLEAR and Identity. Two further metrics address the requirements of agricultural use.

\paragraph{Count error.} Yield estimation from video counts tracks. The count error
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CE}=100\cdot\frac{|\mathrm{IDs}_{\text{pred}}|-|\mathrm{IDs}_{\text{GT}}|}{|\mathrm{IDs}_{\text{GT}}|}
\end{equation}
is the relative over-count (positive) or under-count (negative) of distinct identities; every identity switch that issues a new identity adds to it.

\paragraph{Revisit identity preservation.} A revisit event is a ground-truth object that reappears after at least $G=10$ consecutive frames out of view. At each event, the object's tracker identity is read, using exactly the per-frame assignment that CLEAR makes, at the first frame within a grace window of $W=5$ frames in which it is matched; the grace window allows for track confirmation after reappearance. The event is \emph{preserved} if that identity equals the one the object carried before the absence, a \emph{switch} if it differs, and \emph{unmatched} if the object is not matched within the window. The revisit identity preservation rate is
\begin{equation}
\RIP = 100\cdot\frac{\#\text{preserved}}{\#\text{preserved}+\#\text{switch}}.
\end{equation}
Because the per-frame assignment is CLEAR's own, every revisit switch is one of CLEAR's IDSW, so RIP measures the share of the hardest identity decisions, those after an absence, that a tracker gets right. Revisit outcomes are also reported as a function of absence length (Sect.~\ref{sec:res-revisit}).

\subsection{Identity persistence across revisits}\label{sec:res-revisit}

Fig.~\ref{fig:gap} breaks revisit outcomes down by how long the object was out of view. The baselines preserve identity only for short absences: 57.7\% (BoT-SORT), 74.8\% (StrongSORT) and 61.7\% (Deep OC-SORT) of events with a gap below 30 frames, and at most 3.0\% beyond, because a track lost for longer than its buffer is deleted. MapSORT preserves between 85.6\% and 93.8\% of events in every bin, and its rate does not fall with the length of the absence: it is 93.8\% for absences of 120 frames or more. This is the behaviour Sect.~\ref{sec:dormant} is designed to produce: the landmark of a stationary object does not age, so a dormant track is as recoverable after a long absence as after a short one.

\subsection{Ablation study}\label{sec:res-ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} adds MapSORT's components one at a time to BoT-SORT (a). Keeping lost tracks dormant and reviving them by the Mahalanobis gate on their 3D landmarks (b) raises pooled revisit preservation from 6.9\% to 67.3\%. Its effect depends on how well the landmarks are supported. On LettuceMOTS 0011, whose landmarks rest on a median of 79 map points, the 3D gate alone reaches the best HOTA of all variants, 97.0. On MOrangeT V01, with two points per landmark, it reaches 92.2, and on GrapeMOTS PathPlanning\_7 93.4. Reviving through the per-frame camera instead (c) raises pooled preservation to 85.1\%, and HOTA to 96.1 on V01 and 97.4 on PathPlanning\_7, because projection removes the depth error that lets adjacent fruit pass a 3D gate; on the planar lettuce scene it is 5.6 points below the 3D gate. Map motion, with the learned offsets that revival then also requires of its candidates (d), raises pooled preservation to 89.0\% and HOTA to 93.3 on lettuce and 97.1 on V01, at a cost of 0.7 HOTA on PathPlanning\_7. On AppleMOTS 0008 every variant stays within 0.02 HOTA of BoT-SORT. \subsection{Sensitivity to the landmark gate}\label{sec:res-gate}

Fig.~\ref{fig:gate} sweeps the landmark gate $\tau$ over two orders of magnitude, from the 95\% quantile of the $\chi^2_3$ distribution (7.81) to 1600. \section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The results show that, for stationary agricultural targets, a scene-map position is the identity cue that survives the conditions under which appearance and image-plane motion fail. The effect is largest exactly where agricultural tracking is hardest. When the camera returns to an object after a long absence, BoT-SORT's identity is lost by construction, and MapSORT recovers it at a rate that does not decay with the length of the absence (Fig.~\ref{fig:gap}). Fig.~\ref{fig:timelines} shows the same thing object by object on the four representative sequences: each row is a ground-truth object, coloured by whether the tracker still gives it the identity it first received. The datasets exercise the cue in different ways, and the camera that turns it into a prediction behaves differently on each.

\paragraph{The per-frame camera.} Everything MapSORT adds rests on the camera fitted in each frame, and that camera is almost always available: one is accepted in 99.0\% to 99.8\% of the frames of each representative sequence and of AppleMOTS 0007. The tracks matched in a frame agree with its camera to a median of 0.03 to 0.06 box sizes, with 90th percentiles of 0.12 to 0.17, once their learned offset is added. The landmark projection alone leaves median errors of 0.08 to 0.17 box sizes: a landmark is the median of the map points in its box, not the box centre, and the offset, which learns where in the box it sits, cuts the error by a factor of about three. These errors are small against the spacing of the objects, a median of 0.95 to 3.4 box sizes to the nearest neighbour, which is why a revival gate of one box size rarely admits the wrong object. Fig.~\ref{fig:camera} shows where the camera places the lost and dormant tracks in one frame of each sequence, including those outside the view.

\paragraph{Repeated passes over a row.} LettuceMOTS is the operating pattern of a field robot working a row and returning, and the clearest case for the cue: on all three sequences MapSORT preserves at least 95\% of the returns, with 0, 3 and 2 identity switches against BoT-SORT's 28, 26 and 24, where no baseline makes fewer than 20, and it counts the 40 plants of 0003 exactly. On the return pass of 0011 BoT-SORT gives every plant a new identity; MapSORT changes the identity of two (Fig.~\ref{fig:timelines}). Two parts of the method matter here. The field is close to a plane, on which a full projective camera is ill-conditioned; fitting the plane's homography instead keeps the camera stable. And a returning plant enters the image at its edge, where the detector sees only a sliver of it for the first frames. The camera places the plants of the outbound pass beyond that edge before the robot reaches them (Fig.~\ref{fig:camera}a); comparing clipped predictions and holding a new track at the edge until the plant can be revived is what lets it reclaim its identity before a new one is issued. The ablation also shows that the dense map support of a lettuce field makes the 3D landmark gate alone very effective there, more than the camera-based revival; MapSORT uses the 3D gate only as a fallback, in favour of a single configuration that also serves the sparse orange landmarks.

\paragraph{Sweeps around a canopy.} MOrangeT is the largest and most revisit-heavy dataset, with 950 revisits over ten sequences. A handheld sweep moves the camera close to a three-dimensional canopy, so image-plane motion is dominated by parallax that a global camera-motion warp cannot follow, and fruit go out of view behind leaves and the trunk for hundreds of frames. MapSORT preserves 91.8\% of these revisits and has fewer identity switches than every baseline on every sequence, 90 in total against 829 for the best baseline, Deep OC-SORT. On V01, BoT-SORT issues 213 identities for 110 oranges and MapSORT 119 (Fig.~\ref{fig:timelines}). Its landmarks there rest on a median of two map points, so a 3D comparison is unreliable. Projected through a camera fitted to many landmarks at once, they place the matched oranges to a median of 0.05 box sizes, while neighbouring oranges are a median of 3.4 box sizes apart. Fruit that leave the view at one edge as the phone sweeps on are placed just beyond it until the sweep brings them back (Fig.~\ref{fig:camera}b).

\paragraph{Multi-perspective UAV inspection.} On GrapeMOTS the landmarks of the bunches along a vine lie close to a line, which constrains the camera poorly across it. MapSORT still preserves 78.3\% of the revisits of PathPlanning\_7, of which the best baseline preserves 4.3\%, counts its 65 bunches exactly, and makes no more identity switches than any baseline on every sequence. The camera places the bunches along the vine, including those not detected in the frame (Fig.~\ref{fig:camera}c). On PathPlanning\_3, however, its HOTA and IDF1 are below those of all three baselines, and Fig.~\ref{fig:failures}a shows why. A large bunch and three small ones are tracked from the start of the sequence until a fast, motion-blurred pan takes them out of view at frames 88 to 94; they return at frames 156 to 179. The ground truth gives the four bunches new identities, 20 to 23, so the sequence contains no revisit events, while MapSORT gives them back the identities they had. In ground-truth terms each such link merges two objects, which the identity metrics penalise without counting an identity switch. Annotation policies for when an object seen again counts as the same object are not uniform across datasets, and a tracker built to recover identity across absences is, by design, at odds with a policy that resets it.

\paragraph{Dense fruit without revisits.} AppleMOTS has no revisits, and its fruit are small, numerous and alike, so it tests whether the extra stages hurt short-term association. They do not: MapSORT has the highest HOTA on three of four sequences, and fewer identity switches than every baseline on three. Without revisits, the camera only places the apples the sweep has passed beyond the edge of the view (Fig.~\ref{fig:camera}d), where their tracks stay dormant; 111 of the 164 tracks of 0008 end the sequence in the dormant pool. On AppleMOTS 0007 MapSORT is 0.3 HOTA below BoT-SORT although it makes fewer identity switches, 6 against 8. The difference lies in tracks carried onto a different apple: a track whose apple disappears behind foliage is later matched to another apple nearby, which the identity metrics penalise without counting a switch. MapSORT does this 16 times on 0007 and BoT-SORT 11 times, in both cases after absences of 1 to 31 frames, within the lost-track buffer. 0007 is where the camera is least precise: the matched apples agree with it to a median of 0.11 box sizes and a 90th percentile of 0.67, against at most 0.17 on the representative sequences, while neighbouring apples are a median of 1.3 box sizes apart. Seven of the 16 transfers land within one box size of where the camera places the lost track, and appearance does not separate the apples. Fig.~\ref{fig:failures}b shows one, after 21 frames, onto an apple 0.25 box sizes from the prediction. The single identity switch on AppleMOTS 0008, which BoT-SORT also makes, is an apple seen in two frames, out of view for four and then seen again. Its first track never received a landmark, so the camera cannot place it, and BoT-SORT's motion model does not recover it; Deep OC-SORT avoids it, at 2.2 points lower HOTA.

\paragraph{Consequences for precision agriculture.} The most direct consequence is counting. Every identity switch that issues a new identity is a phantom plant or fruit in the count. The baselines over-count by 43\% to 128\% on the representative sequences with revisits, which would make their output unusable as a yield estimate without heavy post-hoc correction; MapSORT's count error there is between 0\% and 8\%. A second consequence is per-plant records. Because a plant keeps its identity across passes, measurements taken on the outbound and return passes, or from different UAV perspectives, attach to the same plant, which is what plant-level phenotyping and selective treatment need. A third is spatial: every MapSORT track carries a position in the scene map, so a track list is also a plant map. Registering the map to a GNSS-referenced frame, as is routine in orchard and field mapping, would turn MapSORT's output into a georeferenced inventory of individual plants or fruit. MapSORT also composes with an existing tracker rather than replacing it: BoT-SORT's association is unchanged and runs first, and the camera stages act only on what it leaves unmatched, so improvements in detectors, appearance models or the base tracker carry over directly.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The camera and revival stages cost throughput: on a CPU, MapSORT runs at 29\% to 56\% of BoT-SORT's frame rate, and building a scene map on a CPU takes from under an hour to several hours. MapSORT's settings are shared across all datasets, but were chosen on the same public sequences on which they are evaluated, since these datasets provide no separate validation split, and the landmark point filters were chosen per sequence on that sequence's own points. Detections without a landmark, such as small fruit at the limit of the map's resolution, benefit only from BoT-SORT's own association, as the switch on AppleMOTS 0008 shows. Among dense, near-identical fruit, a lost track can be matched to a neighbour of its own fruit, as on AppleMOTS 0007. And the cue assumes that the objects do not move: fruit swaying on a branch, or a plant pushed aside by the robot, would carry a landmark that no longer marks where it is.

%% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

MapSORT turns the stationarity of crops and fruit into an identity cue. It gives every detection and track a Gaussian landmark in a structure-from-motion map of the scene, fits a camera to its own tracks in every frame, and uses that camera to re-match the tracks its base tracker misses and to revive tracks, kept dormant rather than deleted, when their objects return, including at the edge of the image. On four public agricultural datasets covering lettuce, oranges, grapes and apples, recorded from a field robot, a handheld camera, a wearable camera and a UAV, MapSORT outperforms StrongSORT, Deep OC-SORT and BoT-SORT, has fewer identity switches than every baseline on 19 of the 21 test sequences, preserves identity across revisits of any length, and produces substantially more accurate counts. MapSORT currently builds its scene map offline. Recent online reconstruction models that recover scene geometry frame by frame from monocular video \citep{wang2025cut3r,murai2025mast3rslam} are a natural drop-in source for the same landmark observations, and extending MapSORT to online operation on a field robot is the subject of future work.
